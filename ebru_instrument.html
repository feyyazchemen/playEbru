signal processing pipeline

frame differencing
Comparing two consecutive camera frames pixel-by-pixel. Where the image has changed (paint landed on water), the difference is bright. Where nothing moved, it's black. The output is a greyscale motion map updated at 30fps.
pixel threshold
The minimum brightness change a pixel must show to be counted as "motion." Setting this too low picks up camera noise and minor lighting fluctuations. Setting it too high misses faint drops. Calibrate it so the motion map is entirely black with no paint in the tray.
zone analysis
The motion frame is divided into 5 equal vertical strips corresponding to pitch registers (left = low, right = high). Each zone's "motion level" is the proportion of its pixels that exceeded the pixel threshold, expressed as 0–1.
exponential smoothing
A running average where recent values count more than older ones: smooth = (previous × 0.7) + (current × 0.3). Removes single-frame noise spikes. Equivalent to TouchDesigner's Filter CHOP with a 1-frame Gaussian.
slope detection
The rate of change of the smoothed zone value between frames. A paint drop impact creates a steep spike (high slope). Paint slowly spreading after impact creates a shallow, gradual rise (low slope). Only steep slopes — impacts — trigger notes. This is the primary mechanism distinguishing drops from diffusion.
debounce
A mandatory silence window enforced after each trigger fires. Without it, one paint drop could trigger several notes: once on impact, again as the ripple spreads, again as the ring expands. Default 180ms collapses the entire ripple event into a single note.
chord mode (brush behaviour)
A horsehair Ebru brush deposits 10–50 tiny droplets across a wide area simultaneously. In single-note mode this collapses to one note (whichever zone peaked highest). In chord mode, all zones that simultaneously cross the slope threshold fire notes together — a brush sweep across three zones produces a three-note chord. The 4-voice polyphony cap limits the maximum chord to 4 notes.
velocity
How hard or fast a note is struck, mapped to volume. In manual mode: mouse cursor speed (pixels/ms) at the moment of click. In camera mode: zone intensity at trigger moment. Faster/harder impact → brighter pixel cluster → higher velocity → louder note.
polyphony
The ability to play multiple notes simultaneously. Limited here to 4 simultaneous voices. When a 5th note triggers, the oldest active voice is silenced (oldest-first steal). Kalimba decay is short enough that this limit is rarely felt in practice.

audio effects

reverb (reverberation)
Simulated room acoustics. The instrument generates a synthetic impulse response — a recording of noise decaying in an imaginary room — and mathematically convolves the audio signal with it. The result sounds as if the note were played in a physical space. Short reverb = small dry room. Long reverb = cathedral.
convolution
The mathematical operation at the heart of reverb. Every sample of the dry signal is multiplied with every sample of the impulse response and summed. The impulse response encodes how the room responds to a single instantaneous sound — by convolving your audio with it, every note inherits the room's acoustic character.
delay
An echo effect. The audio signal is stored and replayed after a fixed time (300ms here). The feedback parameter (38%) sends the delayed signal back into the delay input, creating further echoes — each 38% as loud as the previous. At 38%, you hear 4–5 audible repeats before they fade below perception.
chorus
Two short delay lines (12ms and 27ms) whose delay times are slowly modulated by a low-frequency oscillator (LFO) at 0.45 Hz. The very slight pitch variation this creates gives the impression of multiple instruments playing in unison — the "thickening" effect heard on electric piano and guitar.
LFO (Low Frequency Oscillator)
An oscillator running below the range of hearing (typically 0.1–20 Hz). Rather than producing audible sound, it generates a slow cyclic signal used to modulate other parameters — vibrato rate, filter frequency, delay time. Here used at 0.45 Hz to animate the chorus effect and at 5–5.5 Hz for instrument vibrato.

synthesis methods

oscillator
A circuit (or its digital equivalent) that generates a periodic waveform at a given frequency. Sine wave: pure, smooth — only the fundamental frequency. Triangle wave: slightly hollow — odd harmonics that decrease quickly. Sawtooth wave: buzzy, bright — all harmonics present, decreasing by 1/n.
FM synthesis (Frequency Modulation)
A synthesis method where one oscillator (the modulator) continuously varies the pitch of another (the carrier). The modulation index controls the depth of pitch variation. High index → complex, metallic, inharmonic sound. Low index → near-pure tone. A decaying modulation envelope creates the "metallic attack fading to clarity" characteristic of 1980s digital synthesizers (Yamaha DX7).
inharmonic partials
Overtones that are not integer multiples of the fundamental frequency. Acoustic instruments like bells and marimba bars have inharmonic partials because their vibrating bodies are two-dimensional (plates, bars) rather than one-dimensional (strings, air columns). The FM bell uses a modulation ratio of ×3.5 (not an integer) to generate inharmonic partials, giving it a bell-like quality.
amplitude envelope
How the volume of a note changes over time after it is struck. Described as ADSR: Attack (time to reach peak volume), Decay (fall from peak to sustain level), Sustain (volume while held), Release (fade after released). This instrument uses very fast attack (2–10ms) and exponential decay with no sustain — the note fades immediately like a plucked or struck instrument.
bandpass filter
An audio filter that passes frequencies in a band around a centre frequency and attenuates everything outside that band. The Q parameter controls how narrow the band is. Used in the sitar to emphasise the characteristic resonant "twang" frequencies of a plucked string.

music theory

pentatonic scale
A scale with 5 notes per octave (penta = five). The minor pentatonic (A C D E G) contains no semitone clashes or tritones — intervals that create dissonance. This means any two notes chosen at random from the scale will always sound pleasant together, making it ideal for a generative instrument where notes are selected randomly.
solfège
A system of naming notes using syllables: Do Re Mi Fa Sol La Si (Do). Used across Italian, French, Spanish, Turkish, and other musical traditions. In the fixed-do system (used here), Do always refers to C regardless of the key. In movable-do, Do is the root of whatever key you are in.
mode
A scale derived by starting the major scale on a different degree. Lydian is the major scale starting on the 4th degree; Phrygian starts on the 3rd. Each mode has a different arrangement of whole steps and half steps, giving it a distinct emotional quality. Western music theory recognises 7 modes; non-Western traditions use many more.
tritone
An interval of 6 semitones — exactly half an octave. Historically called diabolus in musica ("the devil in music") because of its strong dissonant quality. The blues note (flat 5th) is a tritone above the root. The Prometheus and octatonic scales both contain multiple tritone relationships.
equal temperament (12-TET)
The tuning system where the octave is divided into 12 equal semitones of 100 cents each. Every semitone is identical. Adopted universally for keyboard instruments from the 18th century because it allows playing in any key without retuning. The tradeoff: pure harmonic intervals (5ths, 3rds) are slightly out of tune compared to just intonation. Turkish makam music uses 53-TET instead.

Ottoman & Turkish music theory

makam (مقام)
The fundamental organisational concept of Ottoman and Turkish classical music — but far more than a scale. A makam specifies: which notes to use, which to emphasise, the characteristic melodic movement (seyir), typical ornaments, phrases that introduce and conclude the makam, and the emotional quality (character/ethos). A Western scale analogy captures perhaps 20% of what a makam is.
seyir (سير)
The prescribed melodic movement of a makam — the path it must travel ascending and descending. Some makams ascend one way and descend differently. Some begin in the lower register and gradually move upward. The seyir is the "grammar" of the makam; this instrument captures the "vocabulary" (notes and intervals) but not the grammar.
53-TET (53 equal temperament)
A tuning system dividing the octave into 53 equal divisions used in Ottoman/Turkish classical music theory (the Arel-Ezgi-Uzdilek system). Each step is called a koma. This system accurately represents the microtonal intervals of makam music that fall between the keys of a piano. This instrument uses direct Hz values calculated from 53-TET ratios.
koma (كوما)
The smallest interval unit in Turkish music theory: 1/53 of an octave, approximately 22.6 cents. Scale degrees in makam theory are specified in komas (e.g. "Segah is 17 komas above the tonic"). A Western semitone is approximately 5 komas; a quarter-tone is approximately 2–3 komas.
Segah (سه گاه)
The neutral 3rd degree in Rast makam. Approximately 383 cents above the root — between the Western minor 3rd (Eb, 300 cents) and major 3rd (E, 400 cents). This note does not exist in Western equal temperament and cannot be played on a standard piano. Here synthesised at 326.4 Hz (above C4 = 261.63 Hz). "Segah" means "third position" in Persian.
Eviç (اوج)
The neutral 7th degree in Rast makam. Approximately 1013 cents above the root — between the Western minor 7th (Bb, 1000 cents) and major 7th (B, 1100 cents). Synthesised here at 462 Hz. "Eviç" means "peak" or "height" in Ottoman Turkish, reflecting its position at the top of the Rast scale.
Büselik (بوسليك)
The characteristic 2nd degree in Uşşak makam — approximately 150 cents above the root A, between the Western Bb (233 Hz) and B (247 Hz). Synthesised here at 239.9 Hz. Its microtonal lowness gives Uşşak its distinctively melancholic, yearning character that differs from both the Western natural minor and Phrygian modes.
Nim Zirgüle (نيم زيرگوله)
A "half zirgüle" — the characteristic 4th degree in Saba makam, approximately a diminished 4th above the root D. Synthesised here at 366.5 Hz (slightly below F# = 369.99 Hz). "Nim" means "half" in Persian — this is a slightly flattened version of the full Zirgüle. The tension of this near-tritone interval defines Saba's sorrowful quality.
kanun (قانون)
Trapezoidal plucked zither central to Ottoman and Arabic classical music. 26 courses of three strings each (78 strings total), played with metal picks (mızraplar) clipped onto the index fingers. Equipped with small levers (mandallar) near the bridge that adjust pitch in koma increments, enabling fluent makam performance. The triple-strung courses produce a distinctive shimmering, choir-like quality — the three strings are very slightly out of tune with each other, creating natural beating and depth. The name comes from the Greek kanón ("rule, law").
at fırçası (horsehair brush)
The traditional Ebru brush made from horsehair bound to a rose thorn stem. When loaded with pigment and held over the tray, dragging or tapping deposits many small droplets rather than a single large one. In chord mode, this instrument treats simultaneous multi-zone motion as a single chord event — a brush sweep across three zones produces a three-note chord whose voicing reflects the physical gesture.

physical setup guide

tray surface
Use the darkest non-reflective surface possible. Black acrylic, matte black wood, or dark fabric stretched flat all work. Reflective surfaces (varnished wood, glass) cause false motion as ambient light shifts. Traditional Ebru trays in dark stained wood are excellent. The water itself should not reflect overhead lights — if it does, the camera will pick that up as motion.
lighting (most important)
Lighting flicker is the single biggest source of false triggers. Most household LED bulbs flicker at 60Hz or 120Hz — invisible to your eye but the camera sees it. Solutions: natural daylight from a north-facing window, professional "flicker-free" LED video panels (sold for video work), or fluorescent tubes with electronic ballasts. Test by pointing the camera at the empty tray for 10 seconds and watching the noise readout — it should stay near zero. If it doesn't, your light is flickering.
light positioning
Light from the side at 45°, not directly overhead. Overhead lighting creates harsh shadows when your hand moves, and reflections off the water. Side lighting illuminates the paint without bouncing back into the camera. Two soft lights from opposite sides cancel each other's shadows. Avoid any single bright point source — use diffusers (a sheet of white paper or fabric in front of the light).
camera settings
Lock everything to manual: exposure, focus, white balance, ISO. Auto-exposure constantly adjusts as your hand enters/exits the frame, creating massive false motion across the entire image. On iPhone use an app like "Halide" or "Pro Camera" to lock these. On Android most camera apps have a Pro mode. After locking, leave the camera alone — even tiny movements of the camera body register as motion across every pixel.
paint selection — detection vs aesthetics
The system detects luminance change, so high-contrast paints on the dark tray work best. White, pale yellow, and fluorescent colors (orange, pink, green, yellow) are easiest to detect. Traditional Ebru pigments (kalemkari) tend toward muted earth tones (sienna, ochre, indigo) which look beautiful but are harder for the camera to see. Best of both worlds: use fluorescent or white as your "trigger" pigment and traditional colors as visual accents. The triggering pigment will produce the music; the others just paint.
UV blacklight option
For performances: use fluorescent UV-reactive paints under a UV blacklight in a darkened room. The paints glow vividly while the dark room reduces ambient light noise. The visual is spectacular and the camera detection is essentially perfect — fluorescent paint against near-black is the maximum-contrast scenario. Most fluorescent acrylic paints (Liquitex Fluorescent series, Golden Fluorescent) work; test with a small UV flashlight first.
brush / hand visibility problem
The fundamental conflict of overhead camera setup: your hand enters between the camera and the tray, blocking the view and creating massive false motion. Three remedies, in order of effectiveness: (1) Use the ROI crop to exclude the side where your hand enters from. If you're right-handed and your hand comes from the right, set ROI right boundary to around 70-80% so the camera ignores that area. (2) Drop paint with the brush held at the edge of the ROI, then retract your hand immediately. The brief moment after impact, before paint diffuses, is when the camera reads the trigger. (3) Mount the camera at a slight angle (15-20° from vertical) so your hand enters less of the frame — but accept that the left-right zone mapping becomes slightly skewed.
calibration sequence
Run this every session, in order: (1) Lock camera settings, position camera, fill tray with clear water. (2) Switch preview to "raw camera" mode to confirm framing — the tray should fill most of the frame. (3) Set the ROI box to just inside the tray edges, excluding any background. (4) Switch preview to "motion only" mode. With absolutely no movement, raise the px threshold until the screen is completely black. This is your noise floor. (5) Drop one paint droplet — a single note should fire. If multiple notes fire from one drop, raise the slope trigger. If no note fires, lower the slope trigger. (6) Lock these values for the session and don't touch them while performing.
